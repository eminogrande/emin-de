## An accidental n=2 experiment, and what the evidence actually says

## TL;DR

1. I had cracked mouth corners and couldn’t sleep. A pharmacist suspected B-vitamin deficiency. I supplemented. The cracks healed, my urine turned neon yellow, and my sleep got noticeably better.
1. My partner took the same high-dose B supplement. She had no cracked lips. Her sleep got **worse** — she couldn’t sleep at all.
1. Opposite outcomes from the same intervention is the most useful data point in this whole story. It rules out “B vitamins improve sleep” as a general claim.
1. The literature predicts exactly this split. B12 has a documented *alerting* effect in healthy subjects, and a 100-person RCT found B-complex made self-rated sleep **worse**. The largest controlled trial of B12 for delayed sleep phase syndrome found **no effect**.
1. Best explanation: the effect depends on baseline status, not on the pill. Correcting a real deficiency helps. Adding excess to someone already replete is stimulating. A U-shaped curve, not a straight line.
1. The cracked mouth corner is the tell. It’s a recognised deficiency sign. I had one; she didn’t. That asymmetry existed *before* either of us took anything.
1. Neon urine proves nothing except that riboflavin got absorbed.
1. Next: blood work for both of us, before supplementing further. Prediction: I was low on something, she wasn’t.

## The setup

I slept badly for weeks. In parallel, one corner of my mouth cracked open and wouldn’t heal. The pharmacist’s guess: B-vitamin deficiency. I started a high-dose B supplement. Within days the crack healed, my urine went fluorescent yellow, and — unexpectedly — I started sleeping well again.

My partner, watching this, took the same supplement. She did not have cracked lips. Her sleep collapsed. She couldn’t fall asleep at all.

Two people. One intervention. Opposite results.

## Why the n=2 matters more than the n=1

A single person improving after taking something is nearly worthless as evidence. Regression to the mean, placebo, seasonal change, and coincidence all explain it equally well.

A **discordant pair** — same intervention, same household, same timeframe, opposite outcomes — is different. It doesn’t tell you the mechanism, but it does something valuable: it kills the simple hypothesis.

“B vitamins help you sleep” is now dead. So is “B vitamins keep you awake.” Neither survives contact with two opposite outcomes.

What’s left is a moderator. Something differed between us that decided the direction of the effect.

## 1. The mouth corner — the strongest clue

Cracked mouth corners are called *angular cheilitis*. Most cases are local: Candida, staph, saliva pooling, contact allergy. But nutritional deficiency accounts for a meaningful share — commonly cited as around a quarter of cases — linked to iron deficiency and to B2 (riboflavin), B3 (niacin), B6 (pyridoxine) and B12 (cobalamin).

The mechanism is boring and plausible: the epithelium at the mouth corners turns over very fast. Take away building blocks or oxygen transport and it atrophies there first.

Standard clinical practice: if antifungal or antibiotic treatment doesn’t work within 2–3 weeks, test full blood count with MCV, iron studies with ferritin, folate, B2, B6, B12, and fasting glucose.

So: the pharmacist was working inside textbook medicine. She was still guessing without a blood value — but she was guessing in the right direction, and the symptom itself is a recognised marker.

**This is the asymmetry.** I had a physical sign of possible deficiency. My partner had none. If baseline status is the moderator, this is the visible evidence for it.

## 2. The neon urine — proves almost nothing

That’s excess riboflavin (B2) being excreted by the kidneys. It shows that more B2 arrived than the body could use.

It does **not** show a deficiency existed. It does not show the dose is right. Neon yellow is an absorption signal, not a diagnostic one.

Worth noting: my partner would have had the same neon urine. It tracks intake, not benefit.

## 3. The sleep — where the literature gets uncomfortable

My expectation was: B vitamins → more melatonin → better sleep. The evidence says something else, and in places the opposite.

## The mechanism research

B12 does measurably act on the circadian clock. In a crossover study with 9 healthy subjects, 3 mg methylcobalamin daily for 4 weeks phase-advanced the 24-hour melatonin rhythm by 1.1 hours — while simultaneously *lowering* mean melatonin levels. Sleep timing itself didn’t change. The authors’ interpretation: B12 increases the light sensitivity of the circadian clock.

A second study in 8 men supported this: after B12, a single bright-light exposure produced a significant phase advance; under placebo it did not.

This is a real physiological effect. But it’s a *timing* effect, not a sedative one. A drug that shifts your clock is not a sleeping pill.

## Where it turns against the intuition

A German study in healthy subjects found that under methylcobalamin: melatonin excretion dropped, night-time activity rose, and **sleep time was significantly reduced**. The authors’ conclusion was that B12 has a psychotropic, alerting effect.

That is my partner’s experience, described in a journal in 1996.

And the decisive trial: Okawa et al. ran a multicentre, double-blind study in 1997 on 50 patients with delayed sleep phase syndrome, giving 3 mg methylcobalamin over 4 weeks. Result: **no significant effect.**

A systematic review of micronutrients and sleep summarises the B12 literature dryly: mixed effects.

## And B6?

The popular mechanism: B6 is a cofactor in tryptophan → serotonin → melatonin. Biochemically correct. Clinically weak.

The largest randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (100 participants, 240 mg B6 before bed, 5 days) found: more dream content recalled — but no significant effect on dream vividness, bizarreness, colour, or on any other sleep variable.

The important finding is buried in the same paper. The **B-complex** arm reported significantly **worse** self-rated sleep quality and significantly more tiredness on waking.

That is not a footnote for us. That is the result, in a randomised trial, that matches what happened to my partner.

## The model that fits both of us

Not a straight line. A **U-shape**.

```
sleep
quality
   ▲
   │        ╭─────╮
   │      ╭─╯     ╰─╮
   │    ╭─╯         ╰─╮
   │  ╭─╯             ╰─╮        ← her (pushed right, past optimum)
   │ ╱                   ╲
   └─────────────────────────────▶  B-vitamin status
     deficient   optimal   excess
        ▲
        └── me (moved right, toward optimum)
```

Same direction of movement along the axis. Opposite effect on sleep, because we started at different points.

This is the standard shape for a nutrient with a therapeutic window, and it is consistent with everything above: deficiency correction helps; megadosing a replete person is stimulating. [assumption — plausible and literature-consistent, but unproven for our specific case without blood values]

It also explains why the RCTs are “mixed.” If you recruit unselected people, you’re averaging improvers and worseners. The mean lands near zero. That is exactly what Okawa 1997 found.

## What we do next

**1. Blood work — both of us, now, before supplementing further**

Supplements distort the values. Test first.

**Prediction, stated in advance so it can be wrong:** I come back low on at least one of iron, B12 or folate. She comes back normal or high. If we both come back normal, the deficiency-correction model is falsified and the answer is placebo, timing, or something else entirely.

**2. She stops, or moves the dose to morning**

If a supplement is causing insomnia, the fix is not persistence. Lower the dose, take it at breakfast, or stop until the blood work is back. There is no argument for pushing through.

**3. Check the B6 dose specifically**

Prolonged high-dose B6 can cause peripheral neuropathy. This is not a hypothetical risk. Both of us read the label and check the actual milligrams against the RDA.

**4. Washout as a control on me**

After blood work: 3–4 weeks off, tracking sleep. If the mouth corner returns and sleep degrades, the deficiency hypothesis gains ground. If nothing happens, it was regression or placebo.

**5. Adjust expectations**

If my values come back normal, B vitamins are not the answer and never were. Then the cause is elsewhere — timing, light exposure, alcohol, breathing. At that point the useful next tool is a sleep tracker with SpO₂, not another pill.
