# The Echoes of Cold War Diplomacy in Romania, Tanzania and Zanzibar and the Legacy of the 1972 Visa Abolition Agreement

![](../../../media/the-echoes-of-cold-war-diplomacy-romania-tanzania-zanzibar-and-the-legacy-of-the/01-04b74700.jpeg)

Think about this. You are a European digital nomad or a tourist in the 2020s and you want a tropical getaway. You look at a map of East Africa and you see the white beaches of Zanzibar. If you have a passport from the UK, France, Germany or almost anywhere else in Europe, you pull out your credit card and pay for a visa.

But if you have a Romanian passport, you just walk through.

## Why a Romanian passport is different

Romania is still the only European country whose people get visa free access to the United Republic of Tanzania, and that includes the semi autonomous islands of Zanzibar. It's not a new tourism idea and it's not some EU trade deal. It's what is left of a strange chapter of the Cold War, and the story has a tough 4x4 in it, two men who ruled like kings, and an oil refinery built so a king could fuel his cars.

## The two monarchs and the 1972 agreement

To get this we have to go back to the spring of 1972. The Cold War was at its peak and the new independent African states were trying to find their way between the capitalist West and the communist East.

Nicolae Ceaușescu was the communist dictator of Romania, and he really wanted a foreign policy that was his own and not Moscow's.1 He wanted Romania to look like a brotherly developing country to the Third World, one that brings industrial aid without the imperial baggage of the West and without the control of the Soviets.1

In March 1972 Ceaușescu went on a big diplomatic tour of Africa and ended up in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania. There he met President Julius Nyerere. People called Nyerere *Mwalimu*, the teacher, and he was the man behind *Ujamaa*, a local kind of African socialism.3

They lived in very different worlds but they had a lot in common. Both ran huge social engineering projects and didn't compromise much, Nyerere with his rural villagization and Ceaușescu with his systematization.3 And because both ruled with total power and for so long, like a dynasty, historians and political analysts often call their bond the "friendship of the two monarchs."

On March 28, 1972 the two countries signed a Visa Abolition Agreement.6 It skipped the usual rules and opened an easy travel path between the two socialist states. And it survived everything. It survived the bloody fall of Ceaușescu in 1989, Romania joining the EU and Tanzania moving to multiparty democracy. The governments that came after just never cancelled it.

## Aru Jeep Chauchenko

A treaty is just ink on paper, but Ceaușescu knew how to leave something you can touch. He was very good at car diplomacy and he brought Romania's best mechanical ambassador with him, the ARO 240 off-road car.7

The ARO, for Auto Romania, was no luxury SUV. It was a simple and tough machine built for the worst roads, and it didn't need fragile electronics.7 On his 1972 visit Ceaușescu gave several brand new ARO 240 jeeps to the leaders of Tanzania and to the revolutionary government of Zanzibar.5

And they were more than a symbol. The jeeps went straight into the East African bush and showed what they were worth there. Local mechanics could fix them easily and soon they were everywhere, a sign of Romanian and Tanzanian cooperation.

Because people linked the cars with the dictator who gave them, the locals in Tanzania and Zanzibar mixed the brand with how his name sounded. "ARO" became "Aru" and "Ceaușescu" became "Chauchenko."

For decades the Aru Jeep Chauchenko drove across the savannah, a piece of Eastern European industry and a new word, both living on under the African sun.5

## Oil, engineers and fuel for the king's cars

But the story doesn't stop in the Tanzanian bush. It goes across the Indian Ocean to the old rulers of Zanzibar, the Sultanate of Oman.

For centuries Zanzibar was the seat of the big Omani Empire, and Oman and the East African coast are still tied together by culture and by people today.8 Romania pushed its hard modernity across the Non-Aligned world, and its most valuable export wasn't the ARO jeep. It was oil know how.

The Romanian School of Petroleum had trained a very skilled workforce, and in the 1970s and 1980s Romania was the second largest exporter of oil machinery in the world.9 The diplomatic stories of that time tell of a special deal where Romanian car pride met Middle Eastern statecraft. The new Sultan of Oman, Qaboos bin Said, needed refined fuel for his royal fleet of cars, so Ceaușescu sent top Romanian engineers to the region to help build oil refineries.

That story shows Ceaușescu's strategy really well. When Romania sent the engineers and the technology to refine crude oil, it wasn't just building factories. It was building deep personal debts between countries. The Romanian engineers who came to the region were part of a big plan to export people and skills, and to trade technical talent for goodwill and raw materials.1

## The loop closes in 2020

And then, in our time, the story came full circle.

In December 2020 the Sultanate of Oman wanted more tourists, so on its own it dropped the entry visa for people from over 100 countries for stays of up to 14 days.10 And Romania was on that list.

So because of the goodwill left over from the car and oil diplomacy of the 1970s and because of today's economic plans, a Romanian passport holder can now travel easily to both old halves of the Omani Empire. There is Zanzibar in East Africa, which Ceaușescu and Nyerere opened in 1972, and there is Oman, the old Arabian homeland, which the Sultanate opened in 2020.

## Who is who

So who is who in this story. Chauchenko is Nicolae Ceaușescu, the former communist leader of Romania. The Aru Jeep is the ARO jeep, the tough Romanian off-road car he gave to Tanzania. The two monarchs is the name for Ceaușescu and Nyerere, because they ruled with total power. Oil diplomacy is Romania being a top global exporter of oil technology, which it used to build refineries and win favour with Middle Eastern royals. And the visa loop is what all these ties left behind, free access for Romanians to Tanzania, Zanzibar and Oman today.

Today thousands of Romanians go to the beaches of Zanzibar and skip the visa queues that hold up the rest of Europe, and without knowing it they are part of the last chapter of a Cold War story. Two monarchs wrote it, an Aru Jeep Chauchenko drove it through the mud, Romanian refinery engineers fuelled it, and the slow weight of diplomatic history kept it alive.
