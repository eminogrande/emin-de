## What you see first

A camera where a face should be. That is the first fact of this picture and it arrives before anything else. A figure sits low in front of a mirror, elbows up, a small silver and black camera pressed to the eye. The lens points straight back at us. Around it, a pale shop unfolds in soft grey: shoes on thin shelves, a round bag, a bag with a handle of beads, a rail with a tulle dress that looks like a cloud someone hung up to dry.

The photograph comes from Emin's older work, published on his old photo site emino.carrd.co under the heading "taking pictures". The date is not known and neither is the place. It reads as a clothing and accessories boutique, the gallery kind where each object gets a shelf to itself. The person in the mirror is most likely the photographer himself, which makes this a self-portrait. I say likely because the camera hides almost the whole face, and that hiding is the point.

## The grid and the seated figure

The shop does the composing. Slim vertical poles run from floor to near ceiling, and thin metal shelves cross them at regular heights. The result is a grid, and the photographer has placed himself in its middle cell. Two poles bracket his body like the sides of a picture frame. A bag with a beaded handle sits just above his head, almost a crown.

The frame splits into three clear zones. On the left, shelves with sandals and two round bags, and at the bottom a heavy black fringed bag. In the centre, the seated figure in a black T-shirt. On the right, a clothes rail with black hangers and that pale tulle dress with puffed sheer sleeves. Left dark, centre dark, right bright. It is a simple rhythm, and it works because the dark masses are different in kind. One is a bag, one is a person, and the bright one is a garment with no body inside it.

The figure sits low, so the camera sits roughly at the height of the middle shelf. That keeps the poles nearly parallel. Shoot from standing height while tilting down and those verticals would lean in. Here they stand straight, and the calm of the picture depends on it.

## The mirror as a second frame

Look at the far right edge. A narrow vertical strip shows something different: speckled stone and pale panels, the real room outside the glass. That strip is the mirror's edge. Everything to its left is reflection. Along the bottom runs a soft pale band, out of focus, which reads as the lower edge of the mirror or a ledge in front of it.

Many mirror photographs hide the mirror and pass the reflection off as the room. This one leaves the seams in. The strip on the right is a small confession that tells you this is a frame inside a frame. Lee Friedlander built a whole way of seeing on such seams, and even here, in a quiet shop, the idea holds.

## Light from a shop ceiling

The light is retail light, made to sell and not to dramatise. It comes from above and slightly in front, most likely recessed ceiling fixtures spread across the room. The evidence is in the shadows. They are short, soft and sit directly under the shelves and bags. There is no hard edge, no window streak, no single hot source.

That softness flatters the merchandise and also the photograph. The tulle keeps its folds. The fringe on the black bag shows individual strands. Small specular highlights land where you would expect them: on the chrome top of the camera, on the watch crystal, on each pearl-like bead of the bag handle and along the metal poles. The brightest point in the frame is the camera's top plate, a small flash of silver at the centre of a black shape.

## The moment of pressing the shutter

This is not a caught moment in the street sense. Nothing is about to happen. The timing here means stillness: sit down, square up to the glass, level the camera, wait until the hands are steady, press.

The hands show how. One hand cradles the lens from below, the other wraps the grip side with fingers over the top. A strap loops around the supporting wrist, and a round watch on a dark strap sits on it. That is a textbook hold, and there is no visible motion blur in fingers, watch or fringe. In a mirror self-portrait the hard part is not the shutter. It is deciding where to sit so the reflection shows what you want. That decision was made with care.

The supporting hand with the watch appears on the left and the grip hand on the right, which matches an unflipped reflection of a right-handed hold. I read the picture as unflipped, though the camera's lettering is too small to confirm it.

## Focus through a mirror

A mirror doubles the distance. If you sit 1.2 metres from the glass, your reflection is about 2.4 metres from your lens. The camera must focus on that reflected image, not on the glass itself. Here it did. The figure, the hands and the camera are the sharpest things in the picture, while the bottom edge of the mirror, which is much closer, melts into a pale blur.

The shelves and the dress sit a little further back in reflected space and are only slightly softer. That points to moderate depth of field. On a small sensor at a few metres, a normal lens at a mid aperture gives exactly this: the subject crisp, the room readable, no strong background blur. Perspective supports a normal focal length too. Edges are straight, there is no stretching in the corners, and the room keeps natural proportions. My reading is something like 35 to 50mm in full-frame terms.

## An exposure reading in numbers

These are estimates with reasons, not facts.

Shop interiors are dim compared to daylight, often somewhere around EV 7 to 8 at ISO 100. At EV 7 and ISO 100, f/2.8 needs about 1/15 s, too slow for a sharp handheld picture of fingers. Raise ISO by three stops to 800 and the same aperture gives about 1/125 s. That is close to what the picture shows: no motion blur, moderate depth of field and fine noise in the dark midtones.

So a plausible set is an aperture of f/2.8 to f/4, a shutter of 1/60 to 1/125 s and ISO 800 to 1600. If the camera was a Micro Four Thirds body with in-body stabilisation, a slower shutter like 1/30 s would also hold for a still subject. The protected highlights in the tulle suggest the exposure was set for the bright end, or pulled down slightly from the meter, and the shadows lifted later.

The clues lean digital. The grain is fine and even, closer to sensor noise or a digital grain filter than to the clumpy texture of high-speed film. The highlights roll off cleanly. ## Where this sits among mirror self-portraits

Photographers have pointed cameras at mirrors almost as long as there have been cameras small enough to hold. The best-known example is Ilse Bing's Self-Portrait in Mirrors from 1931 in Paris, where she aims her Leica at one mirror while a second mirror shows her in profile. The High Museum of Art quotes her line: "I felt the camera grew as an extension of my eyes and moved with me." That sentence could caption this picture too, where the camera has literally replaced the eyes.

Vivian Maier, the Chicago street photographer whose work was found only after her death, made a large number of self-portraits in shop windows and mirrors, often with her camera visible at chest height. Lee Friedlander made reflections and frames within frames into a signature and collected his own appearances in the book The Friedlander Self.

And then there is the phone mirror selfie, the most common photograph of our time. This picture has the casual setting of the one and the deliberate grid of the others.

## Exercises to try with a phone or a camera

First, focus through a mirror. Stand one metre from a mirror and focus on your reflection. Then focus on a speck on the glass. Compare. You will see the reflection is twice as far away as the glass. On a camera, use single-point autofocus on your eye or the camera body, or manual focus with peaking.

Second, find a grid. Shops, libraries and kitchens are full of shelves. Sit so that two uprights frame you, and keep the camera level so the verticals stay parallel.

Third, expose for white fabric. Put a white shirt or curtain in the frame and dial exposure compensation down by a third to two thirds of a stop until it keeps texture. In black and white, lift the shadows afterwards.

On a camera, start around f/4, 1/125 s and auto ISO capped at 3200. On a phone, tap to focus on the reflection, hold your finger to lock it, slide brightness down slightly and shoot in colour so you can convert later with more control. Hold the phone the way the photographer holds the camera here, with both hands and elbows close, and breathe out before you press.

## What could be better

The bottom band is the weakest part of the frame. The soft pale strip of mirror edge takes space without giving much, and it slightly crowds the knee. A small crop from the bottom, or sitting a little higher, would clean it up. The strip on the right is a good idea, but it is so thin it can read as a mistake rather than a choice. Either give it more width or crop it out.

The picture also lacks a point of human contact. The camera hides the face so fully that only a sliver of eye shows. Ilse Bing's self-portrait works partly because her eye appears above the camera, intent and alive. A few centimetres of eye here would give the picture a second centre. Finally, the tonal range could use a touch more depth in the blacks to separate the figure from the mid-grey wall behind his head.

## Why it stays with you

Most photographs pretend no one made them. The person who chose the frame is outside, invisible, gone. A mirror self-portrait breaks that pretence. It puts the maker and the machine at the centre and admits that every picture starts with someone sitting somewhere, holding a small box to one eye.

This one does it gently, in a place built for looking at objects. Bags and shoes wait on shelves to be chosen. A dress hangs, waiting to be filled. And in the middle, the photographer sits among the merchandise and looks back at the room, at himself and at us, through a lens.
