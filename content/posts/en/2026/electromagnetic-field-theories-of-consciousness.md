This part looks at consciousness through electromagnetic fields, neural oscillations and brainwaves. The idea in all of these models is that consciousness isn't just what neurons compute. It's what their electric activity does together, as a field.

## Fields and resonance

David Jones looked at how big electromagnetic patterns in the brain could be the thing that experience runs on. His point is that a single neuron firing is needed but it's not enough. What matters is the field all that firing makes when it hangs together.

That moves the thing you look at from the neuron to the field. And it matters for binding, so how the brain puts separate features together into one experience.

Tam Hunt and Jonathan Schooler have a resonance theory of consciousness. They say it shows up wherever things swing in resonance, in brains, between living things and maybe in all matter. Resonance is the "binding" that pulls small conscious bits together into bigger conscious things.

It's a kind of panpsychism that comes from physics and not from metaphysics. The weak spot is that "resonance" is so broad that you may not be able to prove the theory wrong.

## Brainwaves and gamma sync

Brainwave approaches are the big group of theories that link a certain wave pattern to a certain state of mind. Alpha waves (~10 Hz) go with relaxed awareness, gamma (~40 Hz) with focused attention and binding, and theta with memory consolidation.

The data here is strong, because EEG, MEG and ECoG keep showing waves that go along with consciousness. But the gap is that correlation isn't causation. And people still fight about which band matters and why.

Wolf Singer and Lucia Melloni say gamma-band synchronization (~30 to 70 Hz) between brain regions that are far apart is how we get conscious perception. When separate groups of neurons swing in phase, what they put out gets bound into one thing you perceive. When they fire out of sync, it stays unconscious.

Tests on visual binding back this up a lot. The critique is that gamma sync may just show attention or how hard the brain works, and not consciousness itself.

## Pockett and McFadden, consciousness as the field

Susan Pockett says consciousness literally *is* the electromagnetic field pattern that neural activity makes. It's not a side effect of computing, it's the same thing as certain shapes of the field. So some EM patterns in space and time just are experience, and that's a radical claim.

It's simpler than epiphenomenalism because there's no causal gap, and in principle you can test it. The hard part is to say which field shapes are conscious and which aren't, without going in a circle.

Johnjoe McFadden's Conscious Electromagnetic Information (CEMI) field theory says the brain's EM field is where information comes together. Each neuron adds to the field, and the field feeds back and changes how neurons fire. So consciousness is the integrated information in the field and not in the neurons.

That solves the binding problem in a clean way, because the field is one thing by nature. The weak spot is that nobody is sure yet the feedback from field to neuron is real at the scale it needs.

## Llinás, thalamus loops and ephaptic coupling

Rodolfo Llinás ties consciousness to loops that run between thalamus and cortex, and most of all to 40 Hz gamma waves. The thalamus makes them and they echo through the cortex. The thalamus works like a pacemaker, and consciousness is the ongoing talk between thalamus and cortex.

If you break these loops, with anesthesia, deep sleep or lesions, consciousness goes away. Tests back this up well. The limit is that it looks mostly at the thalamus and cortex and may give too little weight to other parts of the brain.

Ephaptic coupling means neurons talk to each other through local electric fields and not through synapses. Neurons next to each other push on each other through the field outside the cell, with no synapse at all.

That's a mechanism and not a theory of consciousness. But it matters, because it gives the field theories above a real physical channel. If ephaptic effects are strong enough to sync activity across big parts of the brain, EM field theories get a concrete biophysical mechanism.

## Binding by timing

Andreas Engel and Wolf Singer took the temporal binding hypothesis further. Consciousness needs exact timing, so sync, across groups of neurons spread over the brain. Phase-locking in the gamma range binds what different parts of the cortex work on into one object you are aware of.

Their work set up the whole research program on binding by waves. The open debate is if sync causes conscious processing, just goes along with it or comes out of it.

Lewis and MacGregor built computer models of how networks of neurons that swing can make one state that holds for the whole brain. Their work links neuroscience and dynamical systems theory. It shows how small local waves couple and make brain-wide patterns that matter for consciousness.

The value is that it shows order across the whole brain can come from local rules, with no central boss. That backs theories based on fields and on no single center, over Cartesian theater models.

Zhang looks at how wave dynamics relate to how the brain handles information and to conscious states. So phase relationships, cross-frequency coupling and traveling waves. Cross-frequency coupling, like gamma nested in theta, may be how the brain stacks information in layers across time.

That adds a structure in time to the field theories. Consciousness isn't just sync at one frequency, it's a lot of scales that play together.

## What they share

So the electromagnetic and brainwave approaches share one core idea. Consciousness is something the field does and not something one cell does. The strong versions, McFadden and Pockett, say consciousness is the field itself. The weaker versions use waves as correlates or mechanisms.

The data side is mature, and the wave signatures of consciousness are well known. But the gap in the theory is still there. Why *these* field patterns and not others?
