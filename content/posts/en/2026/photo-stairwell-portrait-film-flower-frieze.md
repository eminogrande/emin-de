## What you see first

Her eyes. That is the order of every good portrait and this one keeps it. A woman in a black T-shirt and a black scarf stands on a staircase and looks up into the lens. Her mouth is closed. Her brows are level. The look is steady and a little guarded, the face of someone who has agreed to be photographed but has not agreed to perform.

The eye moves next to her left hand, which holds the strap of a leather bag and the edge of the scarf near her collarbone. A thin gold chain catches light across the black fabric. Then the eye slides to the wall, where a faded painted frieze of lilies, leaves and small five-petalled blossoms runs down at the angle of the stairs. The frieze is the surprise. It turns a plain stairwell into a stage with old wallpaper manners.

We do not know where this is, and the picture does not need us to. It reads as the stairwell of an old apartment building, the kind with a hard-wearing painted lower wall and a decorative border above it, made long before anyone in the frame was born.

## The view from three steps up

You can tell because her head looks slightly large in relation to her body, her eyes tilt up to meet the lens, and the wall's vertical corner at the left leans as verticals do when a camera points downward.

Shooting down on a person is often called a mistake. The textbook says it makes the subject small. Here it does the opposite, because she looks back. The high angle becomes a question and her gaze answers it, so the power in the frame ends up even.

The geometry is simple and strong. The painted border falls from behind her head to the lower right corner. Her right arm, reaching toward an unseen banister, falls to the lower left. Together they draw a triangle with her face at the top. Her eyes sit close to the upper third line, a little left of centre. A large pale area of wall fills the upper right and balances the dark mass of her clothes. The frame is 3:2, the shape of a full 35mm negative, and it looks uncropped.

## Where the light comes from

This is the hardest part of the picture to read, and it is worth showing the reasoning, because it is the reasoning a reader can reuse.

The first guess is direct flash. The face is lit from the front and fairly evenly, there is little shadow on one cheek against the other, and the corners fall into darkness. Those are flash habits. A small flash obeys the inverse-square law, so a wall twice as far away gets a quarter of the light.

But the evidence leans the other way. The brightest part of the frame is not her face. It is the wall up and to the right, well off the lens axis. A flash on the camera would make its hottest patch near the centre of the frame, on whatever is closest. A rough brightness check on the file puts the upper right wall clearly above her face and the upper left wall far darker, though both sit at similar distances from the camera. Flash does not do that. A window does. The highlights on her forehead and nose are broad and soft, not the small hard dots of a bare flash, and there is no crisp shadow outline anywhere on the pale wall.

So the most likely reading is daylight from a stairwell window up and to the right, falling across the wall and onto her face from roughly the camera's direction. A weak fill flash cannot be ruled out. I would put the window reading at about two in three. The catchlights in her eyes are too small in this file to settle it, and they are the first thing I would check in a larger scan.

Highlights on the bright wall roll off into cream without a hard edge, and shadows in the lower left go blue-grey and grainy rather than solid black.

The tonal layout is a diagonal. Light upper right, dark lower left, and she stands on the seam. Roughly a fifth of the frame is near black, a fifth near white, and the rest middle tones. That balance is why the picture reads easily at small size.

## Guessing the exposure

Three clues give a range.

Shutter speed. Her face is sharp and the arm only slightly soft. If this is window light alone, the shutter was probably around 1/60 s, fast enough to hold a still face from a steady hand at 35mm, slow enough to let a moving arm go soft. If a flash fired, the flash froze her and the shutter number matters less.

Aperture. Her eyes are reasonably sharp and the wall frieze behind her is legible but softer. At about one metre with a 35mm lens, f/5.6 gives an acceptably sharp zone from roughly 0.9 to 1.15 metres. A wall a little behind her would sit just outside it, which matches what we see. So f/4 to f/5.6 fits.

Sensitivity. Indoor stairwell daylight is dim. At 1/60 s and f/4 to f/5.6 you need about ISO 400 film, and the visible grain fits a 400 speed colour negative pushed slightly toward underexposure in the shadows. Call it 1/60 s, f/4 to f/5.6, ISO 400, held loosely.

## Film or a filter

The file name says film. The image mostly agrees. The grain is clumpy and uneven, strongest in the slate wall and the black shirt, which is how colour negative grain behaves when shadows get little light. Highlights fade into cream instead of clipping to a flat white edge, a sign of negative film's wide latitude. The colour splits warm in the highlights and cooler in the shadows, a familiar look of consumer colour negative stocks after a lab scan.

The other side deserves a fair hearing. The file is 1,600 pixels wide, a size at which real and added grain are hard to tell apart. There are no frame edges, dust or scratches, and 3:2 is also the native shape of most digital cameras. Phone pictures usually show smeared shadow noise and lifted, flattened shadows, and neither is here. On the balance of clues, film is the likely answer, at roughly seven in ten.

## The tradition it sits in

Three traditions meet in this frame.

Roy DeCarava, photographing in Harlem from the 1940s on, printed dark skin and dark rooms with rich deep tones instead of lifting them to grey. This frame sits in that line in a small way. The face is exposed for itself, and the black clothes and shadows are allowed to stay dark.

Nan Goldin's colour snapshots of friends made the close, unguarded portrait a serious form. The direct look at the camera from about a metre away, the slight roughness, the sense that photographer and subject know each other, all of that belongs to her lineage.

And Seydou Keïta, in his Bamako studio in the 1950s, set sitters against bold patterned fabric, so the backdrop and the person argue with each other. Here the pattern is not fabric but a worn painted frieze, and it argues more quietly. Delicate flowers against a stark black figure is the same tension.

## Exercises to try

Shoot down a stairwell. Stand three or four steps above a friend, ask them to keep walking, say their name and shoot when they look up. Do it ten times. Notice how few frames have both the look and the step.

Use one window and nothing else. Turn off the hall light. Find where the window light lands on a wall and place your subject so it reaches their face too. Watch the corners go dark on their own.

Meter for skin. If your subject has dark skin and wears black, a camera meter reading the whole scene will often push everything too bright. Meter on the face, or on a camera with exposure compensation dial in minus one third to minus two thirds and compare.

Read your own pictures. Take one frame you like and write down a guess for shutter, aperture and ISO, with a reason for each, before you look at the data. Then check. This is the fastest way to learn the exposure triangle.

## Making a frame like this today

On a phone, use the main 1x lens, which is close to 26mm equivalent, and step a little closer than feels natural. Turn the flash off. Tap on her face to set exposure and drag the exposure slider down a step so the bright wall does not burn out. Shoot a burst as she climbs. Afterwards add warmth to the highlights, a touch of cool to the shadows and only light grain.

On a camera, put a 35mm lens on, or a zoom at 35mm. Set ISO 400 to 800, aperture f/4 to f/5.6 and shutter 1/60 to 1/125 s. Focus on her eyes or prefocus at about one metre and wait. On film, load an ISO 400 colour negative and rate it at box speed or one stop slower so the shadows hold some detail.

## What could be better

The bright empty wall in the upper right is the strongest pull in the frame after her eyes, and it competes with them. A slightly darker print of that corner, or a crop of a few percent from the top, would settle the eye on her face faster. The dark sliver at the left edge reads as a bar and could go as well.

I would not crop much. A tight 4:5 portrait would lose the frieze as a line through the frame and the feeling of looking down a real stairwell, and those are half of what the picture is. The left side also falls into mud in the shadows. More exposure, or a scan that holds more shadow detail, would let the left lilies read as clearly as the right blossoms.
