## The look back

A woman stands with her back to you. Then she turns. Her head comes round over her shoulder and her eyes cut sideways back to where the camera is. Her hand rests on top of her shoulder, fingers loose. Very long straight black hair falls down the left side of the frame, almost to the bottom edge.

The look over the shoulder works because it is a moment, not a position. Nobody stands like this for long. You read it as someone who was walking away and turned back for one second.

Look at the thirds grid. The eye that catches the sun sits just above the upper line, a little left of centre. It is not on a power point. It does not need one. In a frame this dark, the brightest small thing with a pupil in it wins, wherever it is.

## The only red

Scan the frame for colour. Green leaves, black hair, brown skin, a little blue sky. And then a few small red ovals on her fingertips.

<figure><img src="/photos/notes/photo-dappled-shade-portrait-bare-shoulder-evening/02-red.webp" width="1100" height="1035" alt="Crop of her hand on her shoulder with the red nails circled" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /><figcaption>The red nails are the only saturated red in the whole frame.</figcaption></figure>

Measured with PIL, the nails are the only saturated red anywhere in the photo. Everything else is green, brown or near black. The mean colour of the whole frame is about 58 red, 51 green and 32 blue, so the picture is warm and dark, but not red. That is why the nails jump.

Chinese has an idiom for exactly this, 万緑叢中紅一点, one red flower among ten thousand greens. It goes back to a poem by the Song dynasty statesman Wang Anshi about a pomegranate flower. Figuratively it means one thing that stands out among many. Here it is literal. A small red in a big green, and it pulls your eye from her face down to her hand and back again.

## Light that leaks through trees

Japanese has a word for this light, komorebi, 木漏れ日. Tree, leaking, sun. Sunlight that leaks through leaves. English needs a whole sentence for it.

<figure><img src="/photos/notes/photo-dappled-shade-portrait-bare-shoulder-evening/03-komorebi.webp" width="1200" height="1200" alt="Crop of her face with an arrow for sun through leaves from the upper left and a box on the leaf shadow edge on her forehead" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /><figcaption>Sun comes through the leaves from the upper left and draws a shadow edge across her forehead.</figcaption></figure>

You can read the direction of the sun from her face. The left half of the face, as you see it, averages about 70 out of 255. The right half averages 51. Her hair is brighter on the left too. So the light comes from your upper left, over her head, and the leaves above break it into patches. One patch lands across her forehead and her near eye. A darker band, the shadow of a leaf or a twig, cuts across the forehead above it.

This is not soft light. The edges of the patches are fairly sharp, because the light is direct sun, not sky. Direct sun at 19:31 also proves something about the day. It was clear enough for the sun to throw hard edges through a tree that late. That is all the weather the photo can prove.

Tagore, writing in his own English in Stray Birds in 1916, has a line on exactly this, thoughts that shimmer along with the leaves. It sits next to this section. Moving leaves make moving light, and you only get the patch where you want it for a few seconds.

## Measuring the sun patches

Your eye gets fooled by patchy light. Numbers do not. Here are four small areas, measured on a scale from 0 for black to 255 for white.

The sunlit patch on her shoulder, just below her fingers, is 234. The lit eye area is 111. The forehead is 71. The shaded cheek is 47. Her hair is about 19.

Those numbers are not linear. Turn them back into real light and the gaps get bigger. The shoulder gets about 2.4 stops more light than the lit eye, so more than five times as much. It gets about 3.7 stops more than the forehead and almost 5 stops more than the cheek. Five stops is thirty two times the light. All of that sits inside one hand span of skin.

This is komorebi in numbers. One patch of direct sun next to open shade, and the camera has to pick. It picked the face, and let the shoulder run hot.

## Forty minutes apart

The same camera made another frame that evening, 40 minutes later, at 20:11. That one is a portrait under pines in low open sun, written up in A tree does not burn alone. It was shot at 1/8000 s. This one is 1/320 s.

<figure><img src="/photos/notes/photo-dappled-shade-portrait-bare-shoulder-evening/06-hand.webp" width="760" height="737" alt="Pixel level crop of her fingers and red nails on the sunlit shoulder, with crisp knuckle texture" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /><figcaption>At 1/320 s a still hand shows no blur. Knuckle lines stay crisp.</figcaption></figure>

From 1/8000 to 1/320 is 25 times longer, about 4.6 stops. Same ISO, same camera. So either the light was weaker here, or the aperture was smaller, or both. The leaves explain most of it. Here her face is in shade, and shade under a tree is often three to four stops darker than open sun.

You can estimate the aperture with Sunny 16. In full sun at ISO 100 you get a good exposure at 1/100 s and f/16. At 1/320 s that becomes about f/9. But her face is not in full sun. It is in leaf shade, and it came out a little under middle grey, so the exposure was set for the shade. Shade under a tree is often three to four stops below sun. Open up three to four stops from f/9 and you land at roughly f/2.8 to f/4. The sunlit shoulder agrees. It came out about 2.2 stops above middle grey, which is what happens when you expose for shade and a patch of sun sneaks in.

That is an estimate with reasons, not a reading. The blurred discs in the background lean wider. If she stood about a metre and a half away, a 25mm lens wide open would draw discs of around 90 pixels on this sensor, and one disc here measures about 115. So the true aperture could be wider than f/2.8, with the face simply exposed a bit darker. The lens reports nothing, so the file cannot settle it. What is sure is that at 1/320 s a still hand does not blur. The crop above shows clean knuckle lines.

## A Thursday in June

12 June 2025 was a Thursday. The date carries more than this picture.

<figure><img src="/photos/notes/photo-dappled-shade-portrait-bare-shoulder-evening/09-face.webp" width="1000" height="1255" alt="Crop of her face looking back at the camera, one eye in a patch of sun" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /><figcaption>One eye in the sun, one in the shade. A diary page with a timestamp and no names.</figcaption></figure>

On 12 June 1942, Anne Frank turned 13. One of her presents was a red and white checkered autograph book. She used it as her diary and wrote it as letters to Kitty. Almost two years later, on 7 March 1944, she wrote that you should think of the beauty still around you. The line in one translation sits next to this section. A photo file works a bit like a diary page. It has a date, a time and a moment. It does not have the words.

On 12 June 1987, Ronald Reagan stood in front of the Berlin Wall and said, "Mr. Gorbachev, tear down this wall!" Two years later it came down. A tree is a softer kind of wall. It stops most of the sun, and the gaps decide where the light gets through.

On 12 June 1967, the US Supreme Court decided Loving v. Virginia and struck down the bans on marriage across racial lines. Richard Loving had sent one message to the court through his lawyer, "tell the Court I love my wife". People in the US mark 12 June as Loving Day.

None of this is in the picture. The date is the only link.

## The moon nobody saw

The full moon of June 2025 was on 11 June at 07:44 UTC, the night before this picture. People called it the Strawberry Moon. It was also the lowest riding full moon since 2006, because the moon was close to its major lunar standstill, an 18.6 year cycle.

On the evening of 12 June the moon was still about 97 percent lit, and it rose after sunset. So at 19:31 it was not in this sky. The top of the frame shows only leaves and pale blue gaps.

The other big date was nine days away. The solstice fell on 21 June 2025. These are the longest evenings of the year, and you can see it in the file. At 19:31 the sun is still strong enough to throw hard patches. Forty minutes later it was still low and bright enough for the 1/8000 s frame under the pines.

Japanese has a proverb for this kind of evening, 月に叢雲花に風, tsuki ni murakumo hana ni kaze. Clouds over the moon, wind over flowers. Good things do not last. A sun patch through leaves lasts a few seconds before the wind or the sun moves it.

## The one mistake

The brightest thing in this photo is her shoulder. Not her eye.

<figure><img src="/photos/notes/photo-dappled-shade-portrait-bare-shoulder-evening/12-mistake.webp" width="1200" height="1745" alt="Crop of face and shoulder with the over bright shoulder boxed in red and an arrow pointing to the eye" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /><figcaption>The brightest spot is the shoulder under her fingers. The light belongs on the eye.</figcaption></figure>

Your eye goes to the brightest area first. Here that is the sun patch on the shoulder at 234, and the warm skin of the back below it. The lit eye is 111. So you look at the shoulder, then the nails, and only then find the face. About one percent of all pixels have the red channel clipped at full value, and almost all of them sit on that shoulder and back. With ISO 100 below base, and a 6000K white balance warming everything, that patch runs out of room first.

It is a small mistake. The eye still wins in the end, because it has a catchlight.

There are two fixes. The first one is free. Move half a step, or ask her to lean a few centimetres, so the sun patch lands on her face and the shoulder falls into shade. Then the brightest thing and the most important thing are the same thing. The second fix is in the edit. Bring down the highlights on the shoulder by about a stop with a brush, and leave the face alone. That rescues the balance, but not the clipped red. That part is gone.

## Try this

Find a tree with small gaps in the leaves, on a clear evening an hour or two before sunset. Walk under it and watch the ground. You will see bright patches move.

<figure><img src="/photos/notes/photo-dappled-shade-portrait-bare-shoulder-evening/13-try.webp" width="1000" height="1333" alt="Full photo with an arrow for the sun from the upper left and a circle on the lit eye" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /><figcaption>Sun from the upper left, one patch on one eye, focus on that eye.</figcaption></figure>

Put your subject so one patch falls across one eye and the forehead. The rest of the face stays in shade. Check the shoulders and arms. If a brighter patch sits there, move a little until it is gone.

Set the camera to manual. Meter on the lit eye and the skin around it, not on the whole frame. If you use spot metering, add about two thirds of a stop so skin stays skin and not grey. Use a fast lens if you have one, but you do not need f/0.95. At f/2.8 the background will still go soft if it is far enough away.

Focus on the lit eye. With a manual lens, take your time and turn the ring slowly. Ask for the look back over the shoulder, and shoot a few frames, because the leaves will move the light between them.

Then leave the dark alone. Do not lift the shadows. Let half the frame go black, and let one eye and one small red carry the picture.
