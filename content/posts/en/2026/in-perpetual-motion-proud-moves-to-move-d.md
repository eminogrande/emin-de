*Note: This interview was originally published in ****proud magazine Berlin**** (*[*www.proud.de*](http://www.proud.de)*) in ****2010****.*

Life, love, and music in a perpetual motion machine. To be constantly in motion — just don’t stand still — also means letting yourself be newly inspired time and time again, and under no circumstances losing sight (or sound) of your passion. It’s all the easier when inspiration, passion, and vocation join forces. Move D and Jus Ed also joined forces in early November at The Jam Session in the Tape Club — kinetic depth therapy despite the wintery heralds. Movement in music, music in movement. Reason enough to seek a conversation.

Two hands, a DJ booth, and a magenta card changing owners. I move on. On it goes. Two weeks later, an email.

***Subject:**** Your card… ****Content:*** …just showed up when emptying my pockets. I think you gave it to me at the Tape session with Jus Ed in Berlin. As far as I remember, you said something about doing an interview — well, here I am…* Move D is from Heidelberg, and for now, he’s staying there. Two weeks later, however, he’s playing at *Kleine Reise*. I make my way there. How fitting. Don’t you think?*

**Why are we actually speaking English with each other? Do people often think you’re not from Germany because of your English stage name? You’ve actually been around for ages, haven’t you?** Yes. I’ve been around for quite a long time. I made my first record in 1990. The very, very first one even in 1983. In itself, I’m actually rather unknown.

**So you don’t expect people to recognize you.** No. Inherently, that’s the whole idea behind techno, that people don’t recognize you.

**But your music seems to have a high recognition value. Why is that? Do you always use the same snare drum?** Maybe not that, but always the same chords, or generally what you value in your music. That might be different for me than for others. Production is of course important to me too, but I have different ideas and different benchmarks than what’s generally accepted. When this minimal [techno] emerged, it was naturally also about good productions, but that’s also the kind of production I couldn’t care less about. If anything, I look more towards the sixties or seventies, handmade. I try more to make that kind of sound.

**Are you someone like Robert Babicz, who owns an arsenal of old equipment and works exclusively with it — a sound purist, so to speak?** I know Robert, and it’s a bit similar with me. I also collect old gear, but on the other hand, I don’t think it’s super relevant. There are people making great music with Reason and just a laptop. That works too. But this hardware does have a certain charm to it. I also grew up with a lot of these devices and really know how they work.

**You grew up in a classical environment, with two classical concert pianists as grandmothers. And then there was your father’s record collection, from Pink Floyd to Kraftwerk.** That was my stepfather, yes. That was particularly important. Even more important than my grandparents.

**So you had a good relationship with your stepfather?** Yes. It happened quite early. My mother married him when I was four. And so he was basically my father. He studied in America and brought a lot of records back from there. I was four in 1970. He had a really great hi-fi system that practically nobody else in Germany had at the time.

**So he was a music lover, or also a music maker?** No. Just a lover. My real father was a trumpet player and mathematician. But my stepfather also had a lot of trust and showed me how the record player worked. I was allowed to do it myself when nobody was home. That was cool. And then, as a four-year-old, I already started picking out records and listening to them over and over again until I basically knew them completely by heart.

**The melodies?** Everything.

**Do you recognize individual musicians by their style?** Others are better at that. Trumpet players are relatively easy. Saxophonists maybe too, and pianists I can recognize maybe five, but then it stops. I might still recognize the drummer, but with the bassist it gets hard, and I’m not a real nerd. The guitar always interested me the most.

**I recognize a lot of harmony in your tracks.** Definitely. And if you wanted to measure why that is, it’s certainly always similar sounds that I like, and guaranteed to be similar chords and progressions. More minor than major.

**Did you also really study music theory?** At first, I taught myself guitar autodidactically, but then I took jazz guitar lessons with two jazz guitarists. Of course, I also went through harmony theory there. I also took an advanced music course in high school. I can decipher sheet music, but I can’t sight-read.

**As a DJ and producer, maybe you don’t even need to.** Besides, it’s always a limitation when you have this knowledge. Carla Bley, for example, once said she would love to be able to forget everything she knows from theory when she writes. The greatest moments, even with the Beatles, are those bold modulations that you wouldn’t easily come up with if you always stick to the rules.

**Do you see yourself in the category of techno?** For me, techno is just the umbrella term. I call everything techno, from drum ’n’ bass to house, to techno-techno, and ultimately even things like Kylie Minogue, current chart music. In principle, those are all completely electronic productions. In the 90s, techno was to a certain extent even a political movement. But as far as the sound goes, I was always far away from what others were doing. Maybe even more so back then than today. In the 90s, I had a residency at *Ultraschall* in Munich. It happened quite often that I was relieved at seven in the morning by Sven Väth. And beforehand, his vassals would come carrying his bags and always speaking of him in the third person: “He’s coming right now,” and such. And then he finally arrived and, right from the first record, pushed everything to the limit, and I almost lost it.

**How do you approach him then?** Always with respect. He comes from a small place near where I’m from. And I know his beginnings were as an animation dancer in really awful village discos. In the 80s, he then landed a massive hit with *Electrica Salsa* and has sunk his teeth into it ever since. I’ve always respected that, but musically I always thought, “For God’s sake, get me out of here.” The punchline to all of this, though, is that things have changed since then, although I think that’s down to him and not just me. Nowadays, he’s a storyteller with big build-ups, long tracks, and variety. I like that quite a bit. Back then, it was Frankfurt techno, just right in your face. Everything clattered and distorted. But as a guy, I always found him interesting and cool. He also had the *chutzpah* to dance on the floor with his girlfriend among the crowd, and she’s two heads taller than him. He’s just swaying away with her, I mean, you need balls for that too.

**What were your beginnings as a DJ like?** A DJ from the area brought me into techno in the first place. But I’ve been DJing since ’87. That was back then in a mainstream disco in a small town in the tourist zone. I played funk, soul, hip hop, and things like *Ain’t Nobody* and the kind of stuff everyone wanted to hear.

**Do you still play songs like that every now and then?** There are your own listening habits, and for me personally, a certain boredom sets in. Despite all my quota-love for deep house — which I’ve always had and still have — it reached a point where it became socially acceptable and could be heard everywhere, which I found exceedingly boring. I’m now looking for other things as well. I partly love what the young English guys are doing right now.

**The dubstep stuff?** Well, sometimes that’s too rigid for me, but Space Dimension Controller is amazing, Floating Points too. They make their own style. The broad masses in England, on the other hand, are basically doing disco edits. That’s sometimes shocking when you listen to the original and realize how little they actually did, but I do play some of those things and think it’s great. I like such little deviations from the rules. Here at *Kleine Reise*, I can sometimes play things around 110 BPM, where you stay under. Otherwise, I always like Rob Hood or Jeff Mills, from whom I recently bought two records again.

**Have you ever been to Detroit?** I’ve only been there once so far, but it was totally beautiful because it was -30°C and everything was snowed in, so you only saw white. A clean white, not so brown and churned up on the street. But you didn’t freeze either because it was so dry. I can certainly imagine that it can be totally ugly there too.

**Were you nervous when you played there?** Well, I always have the mindset from the start that nobody is going to show up anyway. And then I’m rather surprised when someone is actually there. Most of the time, I’m really tired before playing, which is also a form of excitement. Back in the day with the band, I’d fall asleep in the dressing room, just nod off. But I also don’t feel like I have to prove anything. Most producers are totally nice, and some are not nice. But you can’t change that either. Juan Atkins, for example, once talked to me in a pretty stupid way in Denmark, but that was his problem. Maybe he was just having a bad day or a bad phase in his life generally.

**Didn’t you also have a lot of influences from the G.I.s in Heidelberg?** Where I started, the audience consisted of a third G.I.s and a third Turks, both so to speak not exactly the trendy party crowd in Germany. But I felt comfortable there because the Turks knew how to breakdance, and the Americans had really good music going on and brought records with them. Generally, the Americans were a big influence. We also had AFN, or we still do. That was always the best radio in the area. Like, pop music, yes, but not mega-club pop. Good pop music.

**You recently did a mix CD for *Groove*. Was that your first mix CD?** Nobody had simply ever asked me. When *Groove* then asked, I immediately had ideas of what I’d like to do, classics and old stuff, but time and pressure got in the way, also with the licensing. Realistically speaking, I then limited my selection to people I know personally and can ask myself instead of having to go through the label.

**I read in an interview with you that your standard for good music is “You should be able to fall asleep or make love to it.”** Thematically, that’s just always closer to me than “I’m going to tear your hair out,” or something like that. The falling asleep part has to do with the fact that I like making music when there are people around who join in, or also do something else, read something, write, or even paint, and I totally like it when people sleep. Then you think, “Okay, they want to sleep well now and not get nightmares.”

**What else do you do in life besides music?** Nothing.

**Keyword: ‘Space’.** My biological father took me to the cinema to see *2001: A Space Odyssey* when I was four, even though it was rated for six and up. Then I sat there as a little boy in the cinema and only understood half of it, but was completely thrilled. That really struck me or traumatized me. I’ve seen the film countless times since then and know the entire dialogue by heart. I then actually had a science fiction fascination and built my own lunar rovers that moved and made noises, complete with a cockpit.

**So you could have also become a scientist.** I’m certainly very interested in it, but ultimately, for me, that’s just a mental sport, whereas music, that’s more like making love, when it works out.
