## Sunny-sixteen in the woods

To understand why the shutter fired at 1/8000 s, we have to look at the Sunny-16 rule. Under direct midday sunlight, a correct exposure at ISO 100 requires an aperture of f/16 and a shutter speed of 1/100 s.

<figure><img src="/photos/notes/photo-pine-forest-portrait-evening-sun/07-shutter.webp" width="645" height="484" alt="Pixel-level crop of her shoulder and patterned top with razor-sharp hair strands in sunlight" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /><figcaption>The 1/8000 s mechanical shutter freezes every strand of hair in bright evening light without blur.</figcaption></figure>

Moving from 1/100 s to 1/8000 s is a jump of roughly 6.3 stops less exposure time. Counting down from f/16 by 6.3 stops takes you through f/11, f/8, f/5.6, f/4, f/2.8, f/2, down to about f/1.7 in full midday light. At 20:11 in the evening, the low sun has lost about two stops of intensity compared to noon sun. In that softer illumination, balancing 1/8000 s at ISO 100 pushes the necessary aperture all the way open to f/1.4 or f/0.95. The photographer wanted the thin depth of field that f/0.95 provides, and with ISO dialled down to 100, the mechanical shutter had to run at its maximum speed of 1/8000 s.

On Micro Four Thirds with its 2.0 crop factor, a 25mm lens provides the field of view of a 50mm normal lens on full frame, and its depth of field corresponds to f/1.9. At 2.5 meters distance, the sharp zone is barely 35 centimeters deep. If the photographer had stopped down to f/8 for an exposure of 1/125 s, the sharp zone would have expanded to more than two meters, cluttering the background with twigs and bark cracks. The ultra-wide aperture is what makes this a portrait of a person rather than an inventory of a forest.

## The one mistake

No photograph is entirely without flaw, and this frame carries one distinct optical defect. Look at the upper-left corner of the image. The canopy and the sky in that corner fall into deep gloom, dropping to an average brightness of just 8 out of 255.

<figure><img src="/photos/notes/photo-pine-forest-portrait-evening-sun/11-mistake.webp" width="1200" height="900" alt="Crop of the upper left corner with the dark vignetted region boxed in red and labeled with its brightness" loading="lazy" decoding="async" /><figcaption>Severe optical vignetting in the upper-left corner drops canopy sky brightness down to an average of 8.</figcaption></figure>

This darkening is heavy mechanical and optical vignetting, caused by shooting an ultra-fast lens at its widest aperture. When an f/0.95 lens is opened completely, the physical barrel of the lens obstructs peripheral light rays entering at steep angles, resulting in significant light falloff toward the extreme corners of the sensor. In the upper-right corner, patches of blue sky manage to push through, but in the upper-left corner, the combination of dense needles and optical shading plunges the frame into near-blackness.

The fix is straightforward and practical. Stopping down the lens by just one stop, to f/1.4, dramatically improves corner illumination and eliminates the harshest peripheral falloff. Alternatively, applying a lens vignette correction profile during raw processing can lift the darkened corners. If working with the existing file, a modest crop of about 5 percent around the perimeter trims away the darkest corner falloff without compromising the low-angle perspective or crowding the subject.

Open your lens wide to f/1.4 or f/2, lock manual focus on the nearest eye, and point the camera upward so the tree trunks converge over their head. Check your exposure against the sunlit skin rather than the dark background, verify through the viewfinder that your corners are not completely clipped by vignetting, and take the picture.
