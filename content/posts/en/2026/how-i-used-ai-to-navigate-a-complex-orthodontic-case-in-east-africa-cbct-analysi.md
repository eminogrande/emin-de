# How I Used AI to Navigate a Complex Orthodontic Case in East Africa — CBCT Analysis, Provider Vetting, and Getting a Second Opinion Before Braces

![](../../../media/how-i-used-ai-to-navigate-a-complex-orthodontic-case-in-east-africa-cbct-analysi/01-d414fd14.png)

*A step-by-step account of using Claude AI to analyze dental CBCT scans, vet orthodontic providers, prepare for consultation, and make an informed treatment decision — from Arusha, Tanzania to Nairobi, Kenya.*

## The Starting Point: Two Failed Treatments and No Plan

My partner has bilateral ectopic maxillary canines — or so we thought — four premolar extractions from abandoned orthodontic treatment, and 13 months of uncontrolled tooth drift. Two general dentists in Arusha, Tanzania had attempted braces. Both failed. The teeth were worse than before. We had no treatment plan, no specialist, and no idea what to do next.

We had an OPG (orthopantomogram) from Berlin, an iTero digital scan, and an Invisalign simulation that a dentist had proposed. What we didn’t have was someone qualified to interpret all of it and tell us the truth about what was actually going on.

So I started talking to Claude.

## Phase 1: Building the Case File

The first thing the AI did was organize everything we knew into a clinical picture. From uploaded photos, the Berlin OPG, and my description of the treatment history, it built a structured case summary covering the extraction timeline (September/October 2024), the duration of braces (7 months across two providers), the symptoms (loose front teeth, chewing pain at extraction sites), and the 13-month gap with no treatment.

It immediately flagged several things the previous dentists had missed or ignored. The Berlin OPG appeared to show canines that hadn’t descended into the arch. If true, this meant the case was far too complex for general dentists — it would require surgical exposure and bonded traction with fixed appliances, not just aligners or simple braces.

The AI was blunt: “Invisalign alone is inadequate for this case. You need a CBCT scan to see the canines in 3D, and you need a specialist orthodontist, not a general dentist.”

## Phase 2: Finding the Right Provider

We needed an orthodontist in East Africa who could handle a complex case with impacted canines, extraction space management, and a patient based six hours away by bus. The AI searched for specialists in Nairobi and evaluated credentials, not just Google reviews.

It identified Kenya Orthodontics and Dr. Wandia Mwangi-Evans — fellowship from the Royal College of Surgeons of England, MSc in Orthodontics from Cardiff University with distinction, over 8,000 cases and 20+ years of experience. The clinic’s shared-care model (specialist creates the plan and sees the patient every 6–8 weeks, local dentist handles interim adjustments) was specifically suited for distance patients from Tanzania.

The AI prepared a full consultation briefing document — nine sections covering clinical history, radiographic findings, questions for the specialist, and red flags to watch for. It also drafted a professional referral letter we could bring to the appointment.

## Phase 3: Getting the CBCT Scan

We were in Nairobi staying near Karen, so the AI helped locate Karen Dental Clinic in Zamani Business Park — they had a Carestream CS 8200 3D CBCT machine. We confirmed the equipment from photos I sent, and the AI walked me through what to ask for: full maxillary and mandibular CBCT, DICOM file export, and email delivery to both us and the orthodontist.

The scan produced 651 DICOM slices across two series (standard volume and metal artifact reduction). The clinic said we’d need special software to view them. The AI recommended Falcon (formerly Horos Mobile) for iPhone — a free DICOM viewer. I installed it, imported all 651 slices, and suddenly I could scroll through cross-sections of my partner’s skull on my phone.

## Phase 4: Preliminary CBCT Analysis with AI

This is where it got interesting — and where the AI got something wrong, which turned out to be just as educational as when it got things right.

I screen-recorded myself scrolling through the CBCT slices in Falcon and uploaded the video. The AI extracted 192 frames and analyzed them systematically, identifying anatomical landmarks across the axial slices.

It flagged what it believed were bilateral ectopic canines sitting high in the palate, well above the dental arch. It measured the vertical distance across slices and concluded the canines were deeply impacted, requiring surgical exposure. This aligned with what the Berlin OPG had suggested.

But the AI was careful to caveat its reading: “I’m reading compressed video frames on a phone screen recording. This is a preliminary orientation only. The specialist with the actual DICOM files on proper software will see 10x more detail.”

That caveat turned out to be critical.

## Phase 5: The Consultation at Kenya Orthodontics

We went to Kenya Orthodontics with all our records. Dr. Wandia’s team — multiple doctors including the orthodontist — performed a thorough clinical examination. They charted every tooth, measured the extraction gaps (4.5mm right, 5mm left), noted the fluorosis from Arusha’s water supply, checked for TMJ issues, assessed the bite, and reviewed the CBCT on their professional software.

Their assessment: the case was “pretty straightforward.” Estimated 18 months with fixed braces. No mention of surgical exposure or impacted canines.

This contradicted the AI’s CBCT reading. Either the specialist team missed bilateral impacted canines (unlikely — they had the patient in the chair AND the DICOM files on proper software), or the AI’s preliminary read from compressed video frames was wrong.

## Phase 6: Resolving the Discrepancy with MPR Views

Rather than just accepting either conclusion on faith, I used the AI to walk me through the CBCT in a more diagnostic way. Using Falcon’s 2D MPR mode — which shows axial, sagittal, and coronal slices simultaneously with linked crosshairs — I clicked directly on the canine positions in the upper arch.

The AI guided me: “Click on the upper right canine area in the axial view. Take a screenshot. Now click on the left canine. Screenshot.” Each click showed the same anatomical point from three angles at once.

The result was definitive. Both sagittal views showed teeth with long roots extending up into the maxillary bone — the characteristic morphology of erupted canines. Crowns down in the arch, roots going up. Normal position. Not impacted.

What the AI had misidentified as impacted canine crowns in the compressed video were actually canine roots viewed in axial cross-section at higher slice levels. Canines have the longest roots of any tooth, so they appear as dense round structures on axial slices well above the occlusal plane. On compressed phone video, these look identical to impacted crowns.

The AI owned the error immediately: “My earlier CBCT video read was wrong. The specialist with the patient in the chair plus proper DICOM software got it right.”

## Phase 7: Treatment Decision

With the canine question resolved, the treatment plan was clear:

- **Treatment:** Fixed braces (not aligners — more predictable for this complexity level, and fluorosis makes bracket adhesion challenging, so bands on molars instead)
- **Duration:** ~18 months active treatment
- **Visits:** Every 6–8 weeks for adjustments
- **Retention:** Bonded retainers + removable retainers, retainer checks for one year post-treatment
- **Cost:** ~483,000 KES (~$3,700 USD), pay-as-you-go
- **Schedule:** March 13 for cleaning and separators, March 24 for braces fitting

The team explained both braces and Invisalign options transparently, discussed the fluorosis implications, and specifically recommended braces because orthodontist-controlled tooth movement is more predictable than patient-compliance-dependent aligners for a case with this history.

## What I Learned: When AI Helps and When It Doesn’t

**Where AI was invaluable:**

- Organizing a scattered medical history into a coherent clinical picture
- Identifying that the case required specialist care, not general dentistry
- Researching and vetting providers by actual credentials, not marketing
- Preparing consultation documents so we walked in informed
- Explaining dental terminology in plain language throughout
- Guiding me through CBCT viewing software I’d never used
- Teaching me how to use MPR views to verify specific anatomical questions
- Providing a framework for what questions to ask and what red flags to watch

**Where AI got it wrong:**

- Reading CBCT from compressed phone video frames and misidentifying canine roots as impacted crowns
- Overstating confidence in preliminary radiographic findings (though it did caveat repeatedly)

**The critical lesson:** AI analysis of medical imaging from indirect sources (screen recordings, compressed exports) is useful for orientation but unreliable for diagnosis. The specialist with the patient in the chair, hands in the mouth, and DICOM files on calibrated software will always outperform AI reading a phone video. Use AI to prepare, to learn, to ask better questions — but let the qualified human make the diagnosis.

## The Practical Playbook

If you’re facing a complex dental or orthodontic situation, especially in a region where specialist access is limited, here’s what worked for us:

**1. Gather everything.** OPG, photos, digital scans, treatment history with dates. Upload it all and let AI organize it.

**2. Understand your case before the consultation.** AI can explain what ectopic canines are, what premolar extractions mean for space management, why CBCT matters, and what questions to ask. You’ll have a far more productive consultation if you arrive informed.

**3. Get the right imaging.** A 2D OPG is not enough for complex cases. CBCT gives three-dimensional information. Insist on DICOM file export so you own your data.

**4. Vet providers on credentials, not convenience.** Fellowship training, specialist registration, case volume. AI can help research this. Two general dentists failing the same case should tell you something.

**5. Prepare consultation documents.** A structured briefing saves time and ensures nothing gets missed during the appointment. The specialist sees hundreds of patients — make it easy for them to understand your history quickly.

**6. Verify, don’t just trust.** When the AI’s preliminary read contradicted the specialist’s assessment, we didn’t just accept either answer. We used the MPR viewer to check specifically. The specialist was right, but the process of checking taught us more about the case than either opinion alone.

**7. Own your files.** CBCT DICOM files, intraoral STL scans, OPG images — keep copies of everything. They’re your medical data. You may need them for second opinions, insurance, or future providers.

## Final Thought

The technology exists right now for anyone with a smartphone to get an informed second opinion, understand their own medical imaging, vet specialists across borders, and walk into a consultation prepared. It won’t replace the orthodontist’s hands and training. But it levels an information asymmetry that has historically left patients — especially in underserved regions — at the mercy of whoever happens to be closest.

We went from “I don’t know what’s wrong and I don’t know who to trust” to “I understand my case, I’ve verified the diagnosis, and I’m confident in the treatment plan” in 48 hours. That’s not AI replacing medicine. That’s AI making patients better partners in their own care.
