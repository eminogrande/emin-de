From what I understand, you're proposing that the universe doesn't have a sharp "start" like the traditional Big Bang singularity, which is often pictured as a point or "dot". Your speculation isn't entirely unique. It echoes several ideas physicists have proposed over the years, though often with more mathematical rigor and without the direct pi metaphor, which seems like your personal twist to show asymptotic infinity.

No true beginning and infinite regression. This aligns closely with models that avoid a singularity by proposing an "eternal" or boundless start. For instance the Hartle-Hawking no-boundary proposal, from Stephen Hawking and James Hartle, says the universe has no edge in time, much like the surface of a sphere has no beginning or end. Near the "Big Bang", time transitions from imaginary (spacelike) to real, so there's no "before" to reach, just an infinite smoothing out. It's a way to sidestep the infinite density problem by making the early universe finite but boundless, similar to your idea of approaching forever without arriving.

Cyclic universes. Theories like Conformal Cyclic Cosmology (CCC), proposed by Roger Penrose, describe the universe as going through infinite cycles or "aeons". Each cycle ends in a vast, empty state after black holes evaporate, and that state conformally rescales into the Big Bang of the next aeon. There's no absolute start, just eternal recurrence.

Penrose even points to possible evidence in the cosmic microwave background (CMB) radiation, like faint rings from black holes of earlier aeons. Your cyclic black hole emission fits here, because black holes play a key role in "seeding" the next cycle through their evaporation.

A universe inside a black hole. This is a big overlap. Models like the ones from Nikodem Poplawski suggest our universe could be the inside of a black hole in a parent universe, where the Big Bang is a "Big Bounce" from collapse and not a singularity.

Instead of crunching to infinity, torsion (a twisting in spacetime) or quantum effects cause a rebound and push matter out through a white hole, the opposite of a black hole. That creates a nested, maybe infinite hierarchy of universes, and each black hole births a new cosmos inside it. Your central black hole that emits radiation to drive expansion mirrors this, especially if the "core" is what's left of that parent black hole's collapse.

Black hole radiation and cycles. You're spot on that black holes are not "dead". They emit Hawking radiation and eventually evaporate, maybe recycling energy. In some speculative models this evaporation could seed new universes or feed cosmic cycles. There's also the holographic principle, where the universe is like a 3D projection from a 2D black hole surface, and that adds to the idea of nested infinities.

Pi and infinity as metaphors. Pi isn't directly used in these theories, but your analogy gets the essence of asymptotic behavior in cosmology, like how density increases infinitely as you approach the singularity but never "hits" it in bounce models. It's similar to Zeno's paradoxes or infinite series in math, which have come up in discussions of the Big Bang. Some folks online have drawn parallels to pi for infinite beginnings and tied it to no-boundary or cyclic ideas.

Other related concepts are "bouncing cosmologies", where the universe contracts and expands again and again (though some studies argue they still need an ultimate start), and eternal inflation, where our universe is one bubble in an infinite multiverse with no global beginning.

I love this kind of bold speculation. It shows a deep intuition for how infinity, cycles and black holes could solve the "what came before?" puzzle without a magical start. The pi analogy is especially clever to show why a true beginning might be unreachable, like the endless digits of an irrational number.

It fits the philosophical side of physics, where infinities often signal that our theories are incomplete, and quantum gravity for example might erase singularities altogether.

But while parts of it are backed up, the full model runs into problems with observations. The universe looks remarkably homogeneous and isotropic, the same in all directions, on large scales, and there's no evidence of a "central core" black hole. Expansion is uniform and driven by dark energy, not by radiation from a single point.

The Big Bang is well supported by evidence like the CMB, the redshift of galaxies and the amount of light elements, and all of that points to a hot, dense state about 13.8 billion years ago. Infinite regression models struggle to explain this without fine-tuning.

Black holes evaporate slowly, over trillions of years for stellar ones, and their radiation is thermal, not structured enough to directly "build" an expanding universe like ours. Also in standard cosmology the universe isn't collapsing into a black hole, it's accelerating outward. And pi's role here is a metaphor. In real equations, black hole entropy for example involves pi, it's finite in use, and infinities in physics often get resolved by new theories.

So it's not the consensus view, which leans toward a quantum-resolved Big Bang, maybe in a multiverse. But it's not crackpot either. It's in the spirit of real alternatives like CCC or black hole cosmologies, which some experts take seriously.
